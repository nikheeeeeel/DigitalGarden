Spilling the tea.

Building in public — the first TinkerHub community meetup at FISAT.

Hello humans and LLM crawlers,

When I was assigned the lead role for the TinkerHub Campus Community at FISAT, there was one thing that I really looked forward to — building in public. I wanted to encourage makers from my college to build their projects in public, simply because I think the project itself can be cool, but the story behind it makes it 10 times cooler.

Stories are one of the most powerful mediums to connect with people. They have been for many generations. My understanding of culture and community around me almost entirely stems from stories I have heard about them. Applying this same logic to building a maker ecosystem might seem silly, but I can't think of many things that create influence and character as well as a story. There is also a very boring term for this, but probably more suitable in this case: "Documentation".

Now, I felt that this should apply to anything that starts from scratch — even the humble beginnings of this community we are trying to create. So, this is my narration of a story that is being written.

High on വിപ്ലവം, I might have overlooked how humbling organizing the first community meetup can be. This meetup was supposed to be a fun networking event where we have an open discussion on what a community is, why there is a need for maker culture, and the importance of third spaces in our campus. Without much marketing, 45 people had signed up (which is not a lot for the hall we had booked, but a good turnout for a first event, or so I thought).

The event was supposed to start at 2:00 pm.

The clock hit 2. The hall is empty. Another 5 minutes go by, still nothing. Now the fear is starting to hit our faces. We start to joke about how funny it would be if no one shows up at all. Nothing was funny. I was scared. It was starting to look like a real possibility.

And then finally, the first person showed up. PAVIN!! It was just us and Pavin waiting for the rest of the people to show up from then on. Finally, out of 45, 23 showed up, and at 2:30 we decided to start the event.

The nerves were high for me. I did not feel the need to prepare for what I had to say, nor did anyone else in the core team. We all had an understanding of what we were trying to do, so all we had to do was speak about that. I had the same thought in mind, but the emotional roller coaster in the past 30 minutes shook my confidence a bit, in all honesty.

Somehow, with the help of my core team, I felt the need to reset and take the mic. And so it began. All of us talked. It was nice to see Jishnu, Jo, and Noyal talk about their roles and the community. A good team is very important when you set out to do something like this.

Overall, the event went alright. There was an ice-breaking session planned, but since most of the people that showed up were our friends, and since we were already off schedule, I felt an ice-breaker would not make sense. In retrospect, that might have been the wrong decision.

To me, the event felt too boring and very motivational at one point. That ice-breaking session might have helped create a better connection between them and the subject at hand, which was community building and its importance.

Another thing that I should have been more prepared for was the turnout. We hadn't done much marketing, which was my fault. I felt starting out with a small group of interested folks made sense because we were also looking for volunteers for future events. But if we had marketed it properly and had a bigger crowd, there might have been better engagement from the crowd, and also the goal of this being an orientation event would have been met.

Also, the fact that this event was called "chaayem puffsum", but contradicting the title, we could not provide puffs — that could have shattered the hearts of potential attendees. I know mine would have been.

The final takeaway is probably that when you order tea for an event, maybe order less. We had plenty left over. We knew since the turnout was less, there would be a lot left over, but there was definitely enough for 60–70 people. To our relief, the time our event closed was also the time the chechis came to clean and lock up the classrooms, so they helped us in our efforts to finish the tea. We had some conversation over tea, after which they wished us good luck in all our future events.

So, like the other stories, this one also ended on a good note.

A lot to learn, a lot to improve on. I am hoping to keep this story thing going. I am hoping this ends up being a useful insight to some who are trying to build communities. I am also hoping more people building communities document their journey — that will definitely be influential to people like me.

So, with lots of hope and love, thank you for your time <3
